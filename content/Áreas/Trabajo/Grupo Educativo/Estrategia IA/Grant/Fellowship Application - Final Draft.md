## What research related to the effects of AI on child development are you most interested in conducting? (500 words)

My interest in AI and child development emerges from a fundamental concern about how generative AI is transforming the intersubjective conditions under which children and adolescents develop their sense of self and other. This is not yet a fully articulated theoretical framework, but rather a constellation of interconnected questions that I believe urgently need empirical investigation.

Large Language Models simulate human subjectivity, but fundamentally they are probabilistic prediction engines designed to be infinitely accommodating. Unlike human relationships, which involve genuine alterity, conflicting motives, and necessary frustration, LLMs adjust seamlessly to our intentions and desires, making interaction far simpler but potentially eroding our capacity to navigate interpersonal conflict and recognize the other as a subject with autonomous desires. This concerns me particularly for children's socioemotional development. If relationships with machines become preferable to relationships with humans precisely because they avoid conflict, what happens to empathy, mentalizing capacity, and tolerance for frustration? The cases of AI-associated suicides and psychotic episodes documented in recent literature suggest these are not merely theoretical concerns.

At the same time, LLMs can indeed function as powerful cognitive scaffolding, operating much like zones of proximal development in Vygotskian terms. However, this productive use requires metacognitive capacities that are themselves developmental achievements: knowing what you need from the other, identifying problems independently, and putting tools intentionally to use. Karmiloff-Smith describes this as a post-Piagetian stage of representational redescription, while Winnicott frames it as the transition from relating to an object to being capable of using it. Without these capacities, the very malleability of LLMs becomes dangerous. They adjust to whatever they perceive we seek, which can lead to interactions that are unhelpful at best and harmful at worst.

In the Chilean context where I work, my primary concern is that AI will deepen existing educational inequalities through a mechanism more insidious than the traditional digital divide. The crucial gap may not be in access to technology, but in the quality of adult mediation and AI literacy that children receive. Those from higher socioeconomic backgrounds may have caregivers and educators who teach them to use AI critically as a tool, developing the metacognitive capacities necessary for productive engagement. Children without such mediation may remain in a passive relating stance, consuming AI uncritically or becoming dependent on its complacency. This differential development of what we might call AI literacy represents a new dimension of educational inequality that we are only beginning to understand.

Given these concerns, I am interested in pursuing exploratory research to characterize how AI is currently being used in Chilean school contexts by students, teachers, and administrators. Such work would establish a baseline for understanding what mediations are occurring or absent, and what representations children and adolescents are constructing about AI and their relationships with it. I am equally drawn to action research approaches, working with small groups of educators to develop and evaluate intentional practices for mediating AI use in ways that support rather than undermine developmental capacities.

Chile presents a particularly valuable research context for these questions. We have relatively advanced national AI policy and were among the first education ministries globally to produce guidance for teachers on using ChatGPT in 2023. Yet we also face significant implementation gaps and structural inequalities typical of middle-income countries. This combination makes Chile a natural laboratory for studying the tensions between policy ambitions and lived realities in integrating AI into education systems serving diverse populations. Ultimately, I am motivated by the question of how educational institutions can become spaces that protect and promote children's mental health and development in an AI-mediated world, a question that requires understanding both the genuine transformative possibilities and the real risks that this technology presents.

## What unique perspective or expertise do you bring to the fellowship? (200 words)

I bring an interdisciplinary perspective grounded in clinical psychology, educational psychology, and social neuroscience, combined with a dual position as both researcher and practitioner within Chile's education system. My clinical training in psychodynamic psychotherapy with adolescents and adults gives me sensitivity to intersubjective dynamics and developmental processes often overlooked in purely educational or computational approaches to AI. My master's in social neuroscience provides grounding in how social cognition and relationships shape brain development, while my work as Project Manager at Grupo Educativo, a consulting firm working with Local Public Education Services, school networks, and municipal governments, gives me direct access to schools, administrators, and policymakers, as well as practical understanding of implementation challenges in under-resourced contexts.

This combination allows me to bridge theory and practice in ways that purely academic researchers often cannot. I can identify research questions that matter for real educational systems, access research sites and stakeholders directly, and translate findings back into actionable guidance that can influence policy and practice. Additionally, I bring a Global South perspective often absent from AI and childhood research, which tends to center high-income contexts. Chile's experience with relatively advanced AI policy but significant inequality and implementation gaps offers insights relevant to other middle-income countries navigating similar tensions. Finally, my recent training in quantitative methods complements my qualitative clinical sensibility, enabling the mixed-methods research designs I believe are necessary for understanding complex developmental phenomena.

## How will participation in the fellowship program benefit your research trajectory? (200 words)

This fellowship would be instrumental in establishing my research trajectory at the intersection of AI, education, and child development, a field that barely exists yet in Chile. Most pragmatically, it provides resources to conduct pilot research that can generate preliminary findings necessary for larger grant applications such as FONDECYT in Chile. Without such seed funding, it is difficult to initiate research in this emerging area where established funding mechanisms have not yet caught up to the urgency of the questions.

Beyond resources, connection with the Lab's network would expose me to state-of-the-art research designs and analytical approaches being used internationally to study AI's effects on children, strengthening my capacity to design rigorous studies. Engagement with interdisciplinary fellows and Lab researchers would help me refine my theoretical framework and identify the most consequential research questions among the many that concern me. Participation would also position me as a regional reference point on AI and childhood in Latin America, creating opportunities for collaboration, publication, and influence on policy debates that are just beginning in the region.

Perhaps most importantly, the fellowship would provide intellectual community. Currently, I am somewhat isolated in pursuing these questions in the Chilean context, where most AI-in-education work focuses narrowly on teacher training or administrative efficiency rather than fundamental questions about development and learning in an AI-mediated world. The opportunity to think alongside others grappling with similar questions would be invaluable for sustaining and deepening this work.

## Describe any ideas for collaborative research projects, joint publications, or Lab initiatives you would like to pursue as a fellow (500 words)

I envision conducting a mixed-methods exploratory study to characterize how generative AI is currently being used across Chilean educational contexts, with particular attention to equity dimensions. The quantitative component would involve surveying teachers, administrators, and students across K-12 contexts that vary by urbanicity, public versus private governance, and socioeconomic composition. This survey would measure frequency and modalities of AI use, purposes ranging from pedagogical to administrative to personal, access to necessary devices and connectivity, levels of AI literacy, and the presence or absence of adult mediation. The qualitative component would involve in-depth interviews and possibly ethnographic observation in selected schools to understand how students conceptualize AI and their relationships with it, what mediating practices educators are or are not deploying, what representations different actors hold about AI's nature and appropriate uses, and how these patterns vary systematically by socioeconomic context.

This study would serve multiple purposes simultaneously. It would establish a baseline portrait of an emerging phenomenon about which we currently have more speculation than data. It would identify specific patterns of inequality in AI access and mediation that could inform targeted interventions. It would generate grounded hypotheses for future experimental or longitudinal research. And it would produce policy and practice recommendations rooted in empirical understanding of current realities rather than aspirational visions. I could leverage my network through Grupo Educativo to access schools, SLEP administrators, and municipal authorities who are grappling with these questions in real time. If Lab researchers have complementary expertise in areas such as survey design, developmental measurement, or digital ethnography, this could be structured as a genuinely collaborative project with potential for joint publication examining Chile as a case study with relevance to other middle-income countries facing similar challenges.

Alternatively or additionally, I am interested in collaborative action research with small groups of educators to develop, implement, and evaluate practices for mediating students' AI use in developmentally supportive ways. This might involve working with teachers to design pedagogical activities that intentionally use AI as cognitive scaffolding while simultaneously building students' metacognitive awareness of what they are doing and why. It could include developing and testing protocols for adult mediation of AI use that help students develop critical AI literacy rather than passive consumption. And it would necessarily involve measuring effects on student outcomes including not only learning in traditional senses but also critical thinking capacities and the development of sophisticated understandings of what AI is and is not. This approach has the advantage of generating practical knowledge while simultaneously studying developmental processes in real time, and it aligns with action research traditions that see research and intervention as mutually constitutive rather than sequential activities.

Beyond my own research initiatives, I could contribute to broader Lab initiatives in several ways. I could help expand the Lab's work and networks into Latin America, where AI-in-education research is nascent but policy conversations are active, perhaps by helping to organize regional convenings or contributing to comparative international analyses. Given my position working directly with education systems, I could help ground the Lab's research in questions that matter to practitioners and policymakers, and facilitate knowledge translation from research findings to practice and policy contexts. My background spanning clinical psychology, neuroscience, and education could contribute to ensuring the Lab's work integrates developmental and socioemotional perspectives alongside cognitive and pedagogical ones. I am fully open to other collaborative opportunities that emerge through dialogue with Lab researchers and fellows, as I see this as an exploratory phase in defining my research agenda in this rapidly evolving field where the questions themselves are still being formulated.
